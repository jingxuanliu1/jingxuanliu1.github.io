# Graduate portfolio hero analysis

Research date: 2026-08-29

## Method

This review covers 20 real personal websites from students, recent graduates, and early-career researchers in computer science and engineering. Each affiliation was checked against the person’s own biography or a university-hosted profile linked below. “Intro words” is an approximate count of the primary introductory block, because the exact fold changes with viewport size. The assessment is deliberately critical: institutional affiliation is not treated as proof of good design.

## Five recurring patterns among the strongest websites

1. **Identity comes before inventory.** The strongest openings use a name plus one clear technical or personal thesis; they do not try to cover every résumé keyword.
2. **Concrete nouns outperform broad adjectives.** “Distributed systems,” “runtime monitoring,” “compilers for ML,” and “dexterous systems” communicate more than “innovative,” “impactful,” or “passionate.”
3. **Credibility sits next to, not inside, the claim.** A role, lab, project, result, or publication grid supports the introduction without forcing every credential into the headline.
4. **A small human detail creates recall.** Music, outdoor interests, language, design background, or a playful interaction can add warmth when it is brief and genuine.
5. **The next action is obvious.** Strong recruiter-facing sites expose work and a résumé; strong research sites expose publications, a CV, or contact. Three actions are usually enough.

## Five mistakes to avoid

1. Repeating the same idea through adjacent phrases such as “reliable AI,” “trustworthy ML,” “AI infrastructure,” and “ML systems.”
2. Turning the first screen into a company, award, venue, or technology list.
3. Using generic greetings or slogans that could belong to any computer-science student.
4. Hiding the work behind a long biography, or presenting a wall of text before a visitor knows what was built.
5. Adding a visually impressive device that is unrelated to the actual work, too animated to read, or dependent on animation for meaning.

## Patterns that best fit Jingxuan

Jingxuan benefits most from a name-first identity, one plainspoken technical thesis, one concrete description of his build pattern, a short Georgia Tech line, a visible 2027 opportunity note, and exactly three actions. His equivalent of a “human detail” should not be invented leisure interests; it should be his evident engineering preference for tracing behavior and turning failures into reproducible tests.

This direction is authentic because FluxServe measures request handling and bounded behavior, TraceLab stores and replays deterministic evaluation traces, and the PERCLOS work carefully distinguishes raw labels from independent samples. “Inspectable” is therefore a description of the work and its evidence discipline, not a branding adjective.

## Three original concepts

### Concept A — Technical and minimal

- **Headline:** Reliable AI, from request to replay.
- **Supporting copy:** I build model-serving backends and deterministic evaluation tools that make performance, failures, and system behavior easier to measure.
- **Education/focus line:** Georgia Tech BS/MS computer science · reliable AI and backend systems.
- **Opportunity line:** Exploring 2027 software engineering, AI infrastructure, backend, and ML systems roles.
- **Buttons:** Explore Work · Résumé · GitHub
- **Visual concept:** A restrained left-to-right request path ending in a replay loop, with queue, serve, trace, and verify stages.
- **Why it fits:** It compresses FluxServe and TraceLab into a technically legible systems story.
- **Potential weakness:** “Reliable AI” remains close to the existing headline and risks sounding like a category label rather than Jingxuan’s own voice.

### Concept B — Personal and narrative

- **Headline:** I build AI systems you can inspect.
- **Supporting copy:** I like the part after a model works: serving it efficiently, tracing what happened, and turning failures into tests the next run can pass.
- **Education/focus line:** Georgia Tech BS/MS computer science · reliable AI and backend systems.
- **Opportunity line:** Exploring 2027 software engineering, AI infrastructure, backend, and ML systems roles.
- **Buttons:** Explore Work · Résumé · GitHub
- **Visual concept:** An “evidence loop”: a request moves through serve, trace, replay, and verify, with small, verified FluxServe and TraceLab annotations.
- **Why it fits:** The phrase “I like the part after a model works” sounds authored, explains what Jingxuan actually does, and unifies performance, evaluation, and dependable behavior.
- **Potential weakness:** The headline does not itself show scale; the visual and the next section must carry proof.

### Concept C — Evidence-led

- **Headline:** Measured systems. Reproducible results.
- **Supporting copy:** FluxServe reached 4,688 requests/second on a reproducible CPU benchmark; TraceLab evaluates 120 deterministic agent cases without provider calls.
- **Education/focus line:** Georgia Tech BS/MS computer science · AI infrastructure and backend systems.
- **Opportunity line:** Exploring 2027 software engineering, AI infrastructure, backend, and ML systems roles.
- **Buttons:** Read the Case Studies · Résumé · GitHub
- **Visual concept:** Two compact evidence tracks—throughput and deterministic evaluation—converging on an inspectable trace record.
- **Why it fits:** It proves technical credibility immediately with verified results.
- **Potential weakness:** It can feel transactional, and the FluxServe number needs its CPU/non-production qualification to avoid overstating the result.

## Selected direction

**Concept B — Personal and narrative** is the strongest fit. The research consistently favors a clear identity and a specific point of view over keyword density. Concept B keeps the technical thesis, explains the real work in human language, and leaves proof to the original evidence-loop visual and the existing verified evidence section directly below. It is concise enough for a recruiter while remaining credible to an engineer.

## How the final hero differs from the examples

The final hero does not copy a researched headline, layout, color system, portrait treatment, or interaction. It combines an original first-person sentence with a code-native systems diagram derived from Jingxuan’s own project architecture. Unlike the academic sites, it exposes recruiter actions immediately; unlike the recruiter-heavy portfolios, it avoids employer and skill lists; unlike evidence-first examples, it qualifies proof and keeps the person—not the metric—as the headline.
